LLM Under the Hood

A large language model does not read your sentence the way you do. It chops it into pieces, turns the pieces into numbers, lets every piece look at the others, and then guesses one piece at a time. This page follows a short prompt of yours through each of those steps, using real models.

No patient information. Everything you type here goes to a commercial model provider. Use made-up cases only. The starter prompts are fictional.

1. Words become tokens

The model never sees letters or words, only a list of numbered pieces called tokens. Common words are usually one token each. Rare words, and most clinical ones, splinter into several. Different models slice text differently.

Try this: type a long drug name and watch it splinter.

2. Tokens become numbers

Each token is turned into a long list of numbers, called an embedding. Words that are used in similar ways end up near each other. This map shows about 300 everyday and clinical words, flattened from hundreds of dimensions down to two. Any of your tokens that are on the map light up.

Try this: hover a word to see its nearest neighbors. Warfarin should land near heparin.

3. Every word looks at the others

Attention lets each token look back at the tokens before it and decide which ones matter. In these two fixed sentences, the arcs show where a pronoun actually looks in a real model (GPT-2). For your own prompt we can only sketch the shape of the process; those lines are not real weights.

Try this: find the arc from "it" back to "medication". Then check where "she" points in the second sentence.

4. Pick the next word

After all of that, the model does one thing: it scores every token in its vocabulary as a candidate for the next position and picks from the top. The bars are the real scores for your prompt. Temperature controls how often it picks something other than the favorite. The model sounds just as fluent when it is guessing, which is what a hallucination looks like from the inside.

Try this: slide the temperature up, roll a few times, and watch the sentence change.

5. From autocomplete to assistant

A raw language model continues your text; it does not answer you. The assistant behavior comes from extra training and from a hidden instruction called a system prompt. The left pane is a 2022 completion model; the right is the model you picked. Change the system prompt and re-run to see how the framing shifts the answer.

Try this: ask a question in the prompt box and see which pane actually answers it.

6. Compare the eras

Same prompt, three models, side by side. Each pane shows the year the model was released, who made it, whether its weights are open, how long the call took, and what it cost. The differences you see are four years of progress in this field.

Try this: pick a question with a trap in it, such as a recent event, and see which models guess.
